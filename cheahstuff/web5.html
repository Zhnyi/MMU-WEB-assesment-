Careers and professional certifications

[Where hardware knowledge leads]

Computer hardware is the foundation that several IT careers are built on, because almost every IT role eventually depends on knowing what the physical components do and why they fail.

This page introduces three IT careers where hardware knowledge is directly useful, then sets out the certifications and learning pathways that support them. The four are arranged roughly from the most accessible entry point to the most specialised.

[1. IT Support Technician]

What the job involves

An IT support technician installs, maintains, and repairs the computers used across an organisation. The work includes setting up new machines, replacing faulty components, upgrading memory or storage, and diagnosing why a computer has stopped working correctly.

Why hardware knowledge matters here

This is the career most directly built on the content of this website. Diagnosing a fault requires knowing what each component does, because the symptom points to the component. A computer that shuts down under heavy load suggests a cooling or power supply problem, while one that slows down with many programs open suggests insufficient RAM. Without that knowledge, troubleshooting becomes guesswork.

Typical entry route

This is usually the most accessible entry point into IT, since it can be entered with a diploma or a recognised certification rather than requiring an engineering degree. CompTIA A+ is the certification most commonly associated with this role.

[2. Computer Hardware Engineer]

What the job involves

A computer hardware engineer designs and develops the components themselves, such as processors, motherboards, memory modules, and circuit boards. The work involves designing, prototyping, and testing hardware rather than installing or repairing it.

Why hardware knowledge matters here

This role requires the deepest understanding of the components covered on the Learn page, but at the level of how they are built rather than what they do. An engineer designing a motherboard needs to understand exactly how the processor, memory, and expansion slots communicate, not simply that they connect.

Typical entry route

This is the most specialised of the four and normally requires a bachelor's degree in computer engineering, electrical engineering, or electronic engineering. It is a longer route than the other three, but it is also the only one of the four that creates hardware rather than working with hardware that already exists.

[3. System Administrator]

What the job involves

A system administrator manages an organisation's servers and the systems that run on them, including user accounts, backups, security updates, and making sure services stay available.

Why hardware knowledge matters here

Servers are hardware, and they run continuously rather than being shut down at the end of a working day. That makes storage reliability, cooling, and power supply capacity far more important than they are on a desktop computer. A system administrator has to understand the physical limits of the machines they manage in order to plan capacity and prevent failures.

Typical entry route

Like network engineering, this is often reached after experience in IT support. A diploma or degree in information technology is common, supported by certifications such as CompTIA Server+.

Where to find these

  • CompTIA for the A+, Network+, and Server+ certifications.
  • Cisco for the CCNA certification and its associated training.
  • Coursera for the Google IT Support Professional Certificate and similar structured programmes.
  • Malaysian universities and polytechnics for diploma and degree programmes in computer engineering and information technology.

[A recognised learning pathway]

These qualifications are not alternatives to each other. They form a progression, and the order below is the route most commonly followed into hardware-related IT work.

  1. Foundation knowledge. Understand what each hardware component does and how the components depend on each other. This is the level covered by this website and by the early part of a diploma or degree programme.
  2. Entry-level certification. Complete CompTIA A+ or the Google IT Support Professional Certificate to gain a recognised qualification in hardware and troubleshooting.
  3. First role. Work as an IT support technician, where the knowledge is applied to real machines and real faults rather than to coursework.
  4. Specialisation. Move towards networking with Network+ or CCNA, or towards systems and servers with Server+, depending on which part of the work suits you.
  5. Advanced or design work. Continue into senior network, infrastructure, or systems roles, or take the separate degree route into hardware engineering and component design.

The important point is that step one is not optional. Every stage after it assumes an understanding of what the physical components do, which is why hardware remains worth learning properly even for careers that appear to be about software or networks.
